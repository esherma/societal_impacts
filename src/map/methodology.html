Methodology & Sources

What the numbers mean, how they are built, and what they leave out.

What the map shows

Each county gets an AI Exposure Index. It answers one question: how much of the work done in this county is tasks AI can already do? The index is built from two inputs. The first is Anthropic’s occupation scores, which measure how much of each occupation’s tasks people across the US are actually using Claude for. Real usage is the evidence that AI can do a task. The second is Census counts of how many local residents work in each occupation.

The index is not a measure of local AI adoption. The usage data is national, so it says nothing about whether workers in a particular county use AI. It is also not a forecast of job losses. AI that can do a task often helps the worker rather than replacing them.

How to read the numbers

Every index on the map uses the same scale: 100 = the average US worker. A county at 130 has a job mix 30% more exposed than the national average. A county at 70 is 30% less exposed. The white tick on the legend marks 100.

Counties run from 35 (Kalawao County, HI) to 147 (Arlington County, VA). Among counties with at least 25,000 workers, the low end is about 68.

The median county scores 84, below 100. This is expected. The average worker lives in a large metro area, and metros score high. Only 11% of counties are above 100, but those counties hold 47% of US workers.

The map colors run from the 5th to the 95th percentile county. Counties beyond those values get the end colors, so a few extreme counties don’t wash out the rest.

Why rescale? Anthropic’s raw AI score runs from 0 to 1 and the robot score from 0 to 3. On those scales a county shows up as something like 0.087 or 0.45, which is hard to read. Dividing by the national average puts both indices on one familiar scale. This is a linear change, so rankings, colors and correlations are exactly the same as on the raw scale. The national averages used are 0.102 (AI) and 0.394 (robot), taken from the 2020–24 data for the 50 states and DC.

“All counties” vs. “Large counties, 2024”

The job counts come from the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey (ACS), which surveys a sample of households each year. The toggle switches between two versions of that survey:

  • All counties (2020–24, pooled). This version combines five years of responses, which gives enough of them to estimate every county, including small ones. It is the default.
  • Large counties, 2024. This version uses only 2024 responses, so it is more current. A single year has too few responses for small places, so the Census publishes it only for counties with 65,000+ residents (about 840 counties with usable data). Other counties are faded.

Both versions use the same national average as 100, so you can compare a county’s number across the toggle. The typical county scores higher in the 2024 version (median 96 vs. 84) mainly because only larger, more urban counties are included.

How the index is built

The Census reports each county’s workers in 25 job groups. Each group gets an exposure score: the average of Anthropic’s scores for all detailed occupations in that group. A county’s index is the average of those group scores, weighted by how many of its workers are in each group:

County index = Σgroups ( share of county workers in group × group score )

Economists call this a shift-share (or Bartik) measure. It is a standard way to turn a national, occupation-level measure into a local one. The index changes from county to county only through the local mix of jobs. Two counties with the same job mix get the same score, even if workers in one use AI far more. When you click a county, the panel lists each group’s points: its share of workers times its score. The points add up to the county’s index.

The other map layers

Robot exposure

This layer is built the same way, using Anthropic’s robot exposure scores (“What work can robots do?”, September 2026) in place of the AI scores. Each occupation’s physical tasks are rated by where a working robot has already been shown to operate. The ratings run from 0 (nowhere) through a purpose-built cell and a structured site such as a warehouse, up to 3 (messy, open settings such as homes or construction sites). Counties run from 47 (Arlington County, VA) to 203 (Kalawao County, HI). Among counties with 25,000+ workers the top is 143 (St. Joseph County, MI).

AI × Robot

This layer splits counties into national thirds (low, middle, high) on each index and colors the nine combinations. Very few counties are high on both. Across counties, the two indices correlate at −0.86: a place tends to be exposed to one kind of automation or the other.

Some of this reflects real job content. Across individual occupations the correlation is −0.39, and across the 25 groups it is −0.57. The county pattern is stronger because local job mixes vary mostly along one office-work vs. hands-on-work axis. When the robot scores are shuffled at random across groups, the county correlation averages zero, so the pattern is not built into the method.

Neighborhood spread

This layer applies the same AI index to census tracts, which are neighborhoods of about 4,000 people (roughly 84,000 tracts with 200+ workers). For each county it shows how far a typical tract’s index sits from the county’s, in index points (a worker-weighted standard deviation). The median county is about ±10. The most divided counties, such as King County, WA and Santa Clara County, CA, reach about ±34.

Tract numbers come from a smaller sample, so some of their variation is survey noise. That noise is estimated from the Census margins of error and subtracted out. The layer is shown only for counties with 10 or more tracts. In smaller counties the noise usually exceeds the real differences.

After removing noise, about 58% of the variation in AI exposure across tracts lies within counties rather than between them. That rises to 76% in counties with 500,000+ workers. For robot exposure the figures are 69% and 83%. The county map is a useful summary, but inside big metros it hides most of the differences.

Limitations

Broad job groups

The Census county tables use only 25 broad job groups, so very different jobs share one score. For example, computer programmers score about 730 and network support specialists about 31. Both fall in “Computer and mathematical,” which averages to 371.

Simple averages within groups

Each group score treats all of its occupations equally, regardless of how many people hold each one. Weighting by national employment barely changes county rankings (rank correlation 0.97), but it moves some group scores a lot. Office and administrative support, for example, rises from 187 to about 350. The weighted version is computed in the pipeline but not yet used on the map.

Small-county noise

Survey estimates for counties with fewer than about 5,000 workers have wide margins of error, and chance can produce odd job mixes. Dolores County, CO (about 1,300 workers) scores 132 because the survey implies an unusually high share of computer workers. Treat extreme values in very small counties with caution.

Counties are not labor markets

People often work outside the county they live in, and some counties contain very different local economies. San Bernardino County, CA is larger than nine states. The index describes where workers live, not where they work.

Capable is not the same as at risk

A high index means much of the county’s work is tasks that AI can already do. For many of those tasks AI assists workers rather than replacing them. The index shows where change could show up first, not whether it will cost jobs.

Planned additions Next

  • Commuting zones. Group counties into the roughly 700 areas where people actually live and work, which better match real labor markets.
  • Detailed occupations. Use Census microdata (PUMS), which records about 500 occupations instead of 25 groups. It is only available for larger areas (PUMAs), not for counties.
  • Assist vs. automate. Anthropic’s data separates AI that helps with a task from AI that does it. A layer showing that split would clarify which places face more replacement than assistance.
  • Potential vs. current use. Add a layer for where AI could be used based on what it can do (Eloundou et al. 2023). The gap with current use would show where adoption may still be coming.
